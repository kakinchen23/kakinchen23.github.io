Discovery Project: Homemade Wind Tunnel

A hands-on study of aerodynamics using a tunnel I built myself.

The Problem

Aerodynamics is hard to picture because air is invisible. Textbooks describe lift and drag with equations, but I wanted to see and feel how shape changes the way air moves. Professional wind tunnels cost far too much for a student, so my question was: can I build a small, low-cost tunnel at home that still produces meaningful observations about airflow?

This matters because every aircraft, rocket, and even car is shaped with airflow in mind. Engineers test models in wind tunnels long before building the real thing, and I wanted to understand that process from the inside.

The Process

I approached the build with a simple design process: define the goal, research, build, test, and improve. A wind tunnel has a few key parts, and each one solves a specific problem:

  • Fan: moves the air through the tunnel.
  • Flow straightener: a honeycomb-style section that smooths out the swirling air so the flow is more even.
  • Test section: the space where a model is held and observed.
  • Visualization: a way to show the airflow, such as smoke or small strips of thread, so the pattern can be seen and photographed.

Along the way I had to troubleshoot, which is where most of the learning happened. Uneven airflow, vibration, and getting a clear view of the flow all needed fixes. Each problem pushed me to change one thing at a time and test again, the same way real engineers work.

The Outcome

The finished tunnel let me compare how different shapes behave in moving air, and it turned abstract ideas like drag and turbulence into things I could observe directly. Streamlined shapes disturbed the airflow less than blunt ones, which matches what the theory predicts.

My measured results, such as fan speed, which shapes I tested, and what I saw, will be added here before the Discovery Project Showcase.

The biggest lesson was that a small, imperfect experiment can still teach a lot. I also learned that engineering is mostly iteration: build, test, find the flaw, and improve. Next, I’d like to add sensors and use Python to log data, which ties this project straight back to my computer engineering major and to the embedded systems work I want to do.
